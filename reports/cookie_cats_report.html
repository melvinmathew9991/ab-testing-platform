Figures

The engagement metric is one player wide

Raw mean rounds played differ by -2.21% between arms (52.46 vs 51.30). Remove the single most extreme player - one account with 49,854 rounds against a median of 16 - and the difference collapses to -0.08%.

That is why the guardrail is winsorized at the 99th percentile (cap = 493 rounds, computed on pooled data and applied to both arms) before it is tested. Capping keeps every randomised player in their assigned arm; dropping outliers would quietly remove units from one side. The permutation test on the capped metric returns p = 0.607 against the t-test's 0.615 - the parametric approximation holds up here.

How much this experiment could see

With 90,189 players split 44,700/45,489, the smallest day-7 retention change detectable at 80% power was +3.88% relative (+0.0074 absolute). The observed drop of -4.31% is larger than that, which is why it registers.

A 2% relative change - a plausible size for a gate tweak - would have needed about 336,714 players, roughly 3.7x this experiment. Ninety thousand users is not automatically 'enough data'; it is enough for effects of a particular size.

Why there is no segment breakdown

The dataset carries no pre-assignment attributes - no country, device, acquisition channel or tenure. The only other column, rounds played, is measured after assignment and is itself affected by the gate. Splitting results by it would compare groups that the treatment helped define, which manufactures differences rather than finding them. The toolkit supports segmentation (Experiment.analyse_segments, corrected across all slices); this experiment simply has nothing legitimate to segment on.

On the uneven split

The arms are not exactly equal: 44,700 against 45,489, a 49.56/50.44 split with a chi-square p of 0.0086. That would fail a naive 0.05 threshold. The sample-ratio check uses 0.001 deliberately - it runs on every experiment, and at that threshold this imbalance is within what randomisation produces. It is reported rather than buried, and it is the one check that can invalidate everything below it.

What a naive daily check would have done

Replaying the experiment in a random arrival order and testing at eight points, a fixed 0.05 threshold fires at 8 of 8 looks, while the O'Brien-Fleming boundary fires at 6. Repeated testing against a fixed threshold inflates the false positive rate well past 5%; alpha spending is what makes mid-flight checks safe.
